---
title: "The Children Who Learn Pain Before Words"
date: 2026-11-16
description: "On sickle cell disease, superstition, and the terrible things we do when we are afraid."
topic: "Health and humanity"
---

There was a boy who believed the rain made him sick.

Whenever the clouds gathered, he grew quiet. He would pull his blanket over his knees and watch the sky through the hospital window, as though the weather had come looking for him.

He was nine. He had sickle cell disease. And he had already spent more nights in hospital than most people spend in hotels in a lifetime.

His grandmother believed someone had bewitched him.

She had her suspicions about who it was, too. There was a woman in the village who never greeted anyone, and another who had quarrelled with the family years before. Somewhere between the two, his grandmother was certain, lay the explanation for his suffering.

Before the hospital visits became frequent, they had taken him to a traditional healer. Small cuts were made across his back and arms, little openings through which the evil was supposed to leave his body. He was given bitter herbal mixtures to drink, some so strong that they left him vomiting for hours. Once, he arrived at hospital terribly unwell after taking one of them.

The scars remained long after the wounds had healed.

His grandmother called them evidence that they had tried everything.

I suppose, in a way, they had.

Everyone had an explanation. Very few had an answer.

I have often thought about how cruel it is that children must sometimes become experts in their own suffering. They learn the names of medicines before they learn the names of distant countries. They know which veins are easiest to find. They can tell, from the way a nurse approaches the bed, whether another needle is coming.

And somehow, we still expect them to be brave.

The boy's mother rarely complained. She arrived with a small bag containing clothes, biscuits and whatever money she had managed to find. She had become remarkably good at waiting. Waiting for results. Waiting for medicine. Waiting for someone to explain why her son was in pain again.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from having to prove, repeatedly, that someone you love is suffering.

Sickle cell disease is inherited. It is not witchcraft, a punishment from an offended ancestor, or the consequence of something a mother did during pregnancy. No amount of cutting the skin can release it. No bitter concoction can persuade the blood to become something else.

Yet I find it difficult to be angry with the grandmother.

I think of a woman who had watched a child scream through the night, who had been offered explanations by people she trusted, and who had mistaken the promise of a cure for the possibility of one.

Fear makes people believe extraordinary things. Desperation makes them try them.

But ignorance is only part of the story.

There are families who understand the disease perfectly well and still cannot afford the journey to hospital. There are parents who know the importance of regular care but must choose between missing a day's wages and bringing a child for review. There are children whose futures depend, in part, on how much money their parents have.

And there is something deeply unsettling about the geography of suffering. The same illness can mean very different things depending on where a child is born. In one place, a diagnosis opens the door to regular care, screening and medicines. In another, it begins a lifetime of journeys that a family can barely afford. The disease does not recognise borders, but the chances of living well with it often do.

We speak often about the resilience of such families. I wonder whether we have become too comfortable admiring people for surviving things they should never have had to endure.

In my imagined version of the story, the boy eventually goes home. The rain stops. His mother folds his blanket, gathers their belongings, and leads him out through the hospital gates.

I wish I could say that was the end of it.

But the weather will change again. And the next time the clouds gather, he will probably look towards the sky with the same small fear.

Not because the rain is to blame.

But because nobody has yet taught him that the world can be kinder than it has been.

---

*This is a work of fiction informed by the realities of sickle cell disease, harmful traditional practices, stigma and unequal access to healthcare. The characters and events are imagined.*
